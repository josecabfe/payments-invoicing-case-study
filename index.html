Recording a course payment went from 5 minutes to under 1

A consulting firm that also sells specialized training courses, mostly for lawyers, tracked every payment by hand: checking the bank, typing a note into each course spreadsheet, and keeping no list of invoices still to issue. I built a web form that records each payment once and updates the course sheet, the bank sheet and an “invoices to issue” queue at the same time. The team still works in the same spreadsheets, and can still edit them by hand.

The problem

The firm has trained 5,000+ professionals in 190+ courses over 11 years. At that volume, tracking payments by hand doesn’t scale.

What was going wrong

  • Each payment took 4–5 minutes of cross-checking the bank statement against several spreadsheets, and ended up as a free-text note in a cell.
  • Prices are not the same for everyone: new participants pay full price, returning participants pay another, and on top of that come special promotions, personal discounts and group discounts on request. Every payment had to be checked against its own deal, which is a big part of why it took so long.
  • Every course lives in its own spreadsheet, with 1 to 4 installments and slightly different layouts.
  • Typos, a transfer assigned twice or to the wrong person, and payments whose invoice was never issued, because there was no list of what was still pending.
  • When the invoice was finally issued, its number was typed over the note, so the payment details were lost.

What the owner asked for

  • Change our routine as little as possible. Same spreadsheets, same way of reading them.
  • Everything must stay editable by hand, so every promotion and special deal can still be handled.
  • The person who invoices shouldn’t see participant and installment details, only what they need to issue the invoice.
  • No new software costs.

How it works now

One form replaces the cross-checking. Invoices are still written by hand, as before, but nothing gets forgotten.

  1. Record the payment

    Pick course, participant and installment, then the bank transfer from a list of incoming transfers not yet assigned.

  2. Course sheet updated

    The installment cell now reads “Paid – invoice pending”. The payment details are kept as a cell note.

  3. Bank sheet updated

    The transfer is tagged with the course and the participant, so it can’t be used again.

  4. Queued for invoicing

    A card with everything the invoice needs. Who to invoice (name or company, and tax ID) comes straight from the enrollment form.

  5. Invoice written

    By hand, in the pre-printed invoice booklet, exactly as before. Amount, VAT and amount in words are ready to copy.

  6. Number entered once

    It replaces “pending” in the course sheet, fills the bank’s invoice column and leaves the queue.

Invoicing in Paraguay, in short

Every sale needs a legal invoice. This firm uses pre-printed invoice booklets authorized by the tax authority, and every invoice has its own number (e.g. 001-001-0000123). Each invoice carries the tax ID (RUC) of whoever pays, the participant or sometimes their law firm, which they later use to file their own taxes. That’s why the system takes the invoicing details from their own field in the enrollment form.

Recording payments and issuing invoices are two separate, password-protected screens built from the same code, so each person sees only their part. It also handles cash, deposits, partial payments, one invoice covering several payments, and someone paying for someone else.

A deliberate choice: a simple core, editable edges

The two common prices are easy to automate. Promotions, personal discounts and group deals change all the time, and building a rule for each one would have made the system hard to use and hard to maintain.

What the system automates

  • The everyday path: one payment, one transfer, one invoice.
  • The two common prices: new participant (full price) and returning participant. The participant’s profile shows which one applies.
  • Matching each payment to the bank and keeping the three sheets in sync.
  • The checks that stop costly mistakes, like a transfer used twice.

What stays in people’s hands

  • Special cases go under an “Other” category, with a comment that the rest of the team sees, including whoever issues the invoice.
  • Special prices, discounts and group deals: adjusted in the sheet as always.
  • Any cell can be fixed by hand, and the system keeps the other sheets in sync.
  • Rare cases, like cancelling a payment or a credit note, on purpose.

The result: a system that is simple to use, simple to build and maintain, and fits naturally into the work the sales and finance team was already doing.

Mistakes it prevents

The screen guides the user, but every check is repeated on the server, so a mistake can’t slip through even if someone skips the screen.

MistakeHow the system stops it
Same transfer used twiceOnce assigned, a transfer disappears from the list. The server checks again before saving.
Wrong amountThe amount can’t be larger than the bank transfer, and the participant’s profile shows what is still pending.
Payment never invoicedEvery payment lands in “Invoices to issue”, sorted by date, until its number is entered.
Invoice to the wrong name or tax IDInvoicing details come from the enrollment form, not retyped.
Two people saving at onceA lock lets one save at a time. If the installment changed meanwhile, it warns instead of overwriting.
Invoice number mistyped or reusedFormat is checked, and a number already used for another participant is rejected. Fixing it in the sheet fixes it everywhere.
Rows inserted, deleted or sortedEach payment has its own ID. Rows are found by that ID and the participant’s ID number, never by row position.
Columns moved or layouts differColumns are found by their header name, so each course sheet can keep its own layout.

Technical details
  • Backend in Google Apps Script (JavaScript V8): about 1,000 lines and 49 functions. Frontend in plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript, no libraries, responsive.
  • Google Sheets is the database, because the team already works there and the owner needed to keep editing by hand.
  • An installable trigger watches manual edits (invoice number, “done”, amount, date) and keeps the course and bank sheets in sync.
  • LockService prevents simultaneous saves. CacheService handles sessions.
  • Password per screen (payments, invoices or both), 6-hour sessions, a 15-minute lockout after 5 failed attempts, and every server function checks the session.
  • Developed locally with clasp and git (28 commits over two weeks).
