The problem.

“Why does recording one payment take 5 minutes, and still go wrong?”The firm has trained 5,000+ professionals in 190+ courses over 11 years. At that volume, tracking payments by hand doesn’t scale.

What was going wrong

  • Each payment took 4–5 minutes of cross-checking the bank statement against several spreadsheets, and ended up as a free-text note in a cell.
  • Prices vary: new participants pay full price, returning participants pay another, plus promotions, personal discounts and group discounts. Every payment had to be checked against its own deal.
  • Every course lives in its own spreadsheet, with 1 to 4 installments and slightly different layouts.
  • Typos, transfers assigned twice or to the wrong person, and invoices never issued, because nothing listed what was pending.

What the owner asked for

  • Change our routine as little as possible. Same spreadsheets, same way of reading them.
  • Everything must stay editable by hand, so every promotion and special deal can still be handled.
  • The person who invoices shouldn’t see participant details, only what the invoice needs.
  • No new software costs.

How it works now.

“How do we record each payment once and never forget its invoice?”One form replaces the cross-checking. Invoices are still written by hand, as before, but nothing gets forgotten.

  1. Record the payment

    Pick course, participant and installment, then the bank transfer from a list of transfers not yet assigned.

  2. Course sheet updated

    The installment cell reads “Paid – invoice pending”. Payment details are kept as a cell note.

  3. Bank sheet updated

    The transfer is tagged with the course and the participant, so it can’t be used again.

  4. Queued for invoicing

    Who to invoice (name or company, and tax ID) comes straight from the enrollment form.

  5. Invoice written

    By hand, in the pre-printed booklet. Amount, VAT and amount in words are ready to copy.

  6. Number entered once

    It replaces “pending” in the course sheet, fills the bank’s invoice column and leaves the queue.

Invoicing in Paraguay, in short

Every sale needs a legal invoice. This firm uses pre-printed booklets authorized by the tax authority, and every invoice has its own number (e.g. 001-001-0000123). Each invoice carries the tax ID (RUC) of whoever pays, the participant or sometimes their law firm, which they later use to file their own taxes. That’s why the system takes the invoicing details from their own field in the enrollment form.

Recording payments and issuing invoices are two separate, password-protected screens built from the same code. It also handles cash, deposits, partial payments, one invoice for several payments, and someone paying for someone else.

A simple core. Editable edges.

“What about promotions, discounts and special deals?”The two common prices are easy to automate. Promotions and special deals change all the time, and a rule for each one would have made the system hard to use and hard to maintain.

What the system automates

  • The everyday path: one payment, one transfer, one invoice.
  • The two common prices: new participant (full price) and returning participant.
  • Matching each payment to the bank and keeping the three sheets in sync.
  • The checks that stop costly mistakes, like a transfer used twice.

What stays in people’s hands

  • Special cases go under “Other”, with a comment the whole team sees, including whoever invoices.
  • Special prices, discounts and group deals: adjusted in the sheet as always.
  • Any cell can be fixed by hand; the system keeps the other sheets in sync.
  • Rare cases, like cancelling a payment or a credit note, on purpose.

The result: simple to use, simple to maintain, and it fits naturally into the work the sales and finance team was already doing.

Mistakes it prevents.

“What stops a transfer from being used twice, or an invoice from being missed?”The screen guides the user, but every check is repeated on the server, so a mistake can’t slip through even if someone skips the screen.

MistakeHow the system stops it
Same transfer used twiceOnce assigned, a transfer disappears from the list. The server checks again before saving.
Wrong amountThe amount can’t be larger than the bank transfer, and the participant’s profile shows what is still pending.
Payment never invoicedEvery payment lands in “Invoices to issue”, sorted by date, until its number is entered.
Invoice to the wrong name or tax IDInvoicing details come from the enrollment form, not retyped.
Two people saving at onceA lock lets one save at a time. If the installment changed meanwhile, it warns instead of overwriting.
Invoice number mistyped or reusedFormat is checked, and a number already used for another participant is rejected. Fixing it in the sheet fixes it everywhere.
Rows inserted, deleted or sortedEach payment has its own ID. Rows are found by that ID and the participant’s ID number, never by position.
Columns moved or layouts differColumns are found by their header name, so each course sheet can keep its own layout.

What I did.

End to end: from mapping the old process with the team to a tool they use every day.

  • Process mapping with users
  • Requirements from a non-technical owner
  • Financial data validation & reconciliation
  • KPI analysis in Google Sheets
  • User-friendly design
  • Google Apps Script / JavaScript
  • Google Sheets as a database
  • Automated testing
  • Access control
Technical details
  • Payment and invoicing KPIs tracked and analyzed in Google Sheets.
  • Backend in Google Apps Script (JavaScript V8): about 1,000 lines and 49 functions. Frontend in plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript, no libraries, responsive.
  • Google Sheets is the database, because the team already works there and the owner needed to keep editing by hand.
  • An installable trigger watches manual edits (invoice number, “done”, amount, date) and keeps the course and bank sheets in sync.
  • LockService prevents simultaneous saves. CacheService handles sessions.
  • Password per screen (payments, invoices or both), 6-hour sessions, a 15-minute lockout after 5 failed attempts, and every server function checks the session.
  • Developed locally with clasp and git (28 commits over two weeks).
